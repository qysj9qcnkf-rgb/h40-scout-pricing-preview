Why Are Shoppers Leaving My Shopify Store Without Buying?

Shoppers leaving your Shopify store without buying? See the usual causes, why analytics can't tell you the reason, and how to ask the shoppers who left.

Scout guides · 5 min read

Short answerCommon causes are checkout friction, surprise shipping costs and unanswered buying questions, but the fastest way to know for your store is to ask the shoppers who left. Collect their reasons in their own words, group them, and fix the most frequent one first.

What "leaving without buying" looks like in Shopify analytics

You usually notice it as a gap. Sessions keep coming, and products get viewed and even added to carts, but orders don't follow.

Shopify's behavior reports split the journey into steps: sessions, sessions with cart additions, sessions that reached checkout, and sessions that completed checkout. (Shopify Help Center: behavior reports) Where the biggest drop happens is your first clue. Our no-sales audit walks through what each stage points to.

Treat any drop as a starting point, not a verdict. A small sample, one unusual traffic source or a tracking change can all distort the picture. Compare the same product, traffic source and device over comparable dates before drawing conclusions.

The usual suspects: checkout, shipping cost, trust, unanswered questions, slow support

Check these familiar causes against your own store first.

Checkout friction. Forced account creation, a missing payment method, or a form that misbehaves on mobile can all stop a shopper who had already decided to buy. Run a test order on your phone, the way a first-time visitor would.

Shipping cost and delivery surprises. A shipping fee or delivery estimate that only appears at checkout changes the deal. If the page doesn't set expectations early, the cart is where shoppers find out and leave.

Trust. New visitors want to know who you are, what happens if something goes wrong, and whether real people bought before them. Clear return terms, a working contact route and plain product facts do more here than badges.

Unanswered buying questions. "Does this fit my setup?" "What's included?" "How is this different from the cheaper one?" If the page doesn't answer the question a shopper actually has, a shopper may leave to look elsewhere rather than ask.

Slow support. When shoppers do ask, the reply needs to reach them while they're still deciding. A slow answer to a pre-purchase question can end the sale.

Each of these can be the main reason at one store and barely matter at another.

Why analytics can't tell you the reason

Analytics records what shoppers did, not why they did it. A report can show that people left at the cart. It can't tell you whether they left because of the shipping fee, a missing size, a doubt about compatibility or a better price in another tab.

So owners guess. Some redesign the product page, others add an app or rewrite the copy. Each guess costs time, and if it's wrong, the real cause stays in place.

Heatmaps and session recordings narrow the guess, because you can see where people hesitate or tap repeatedly. They still don't show what the shopper was thinking. The only direct way to learn why someone left is to ask them.

How to ask shoppers who left

One short, honest question, put to the right people while their visit is still fresh, is enough.

Pick who to ask. Start with shoppers who showed real intent: people who added to cart, started checkout, or asked a question and didn't order. Their reasons are more useful than those of someone who bounced in two seconds.

Ask one open question. "What stopped you from ordering today?" works better than a multiple-choice list, because a list only offers the reasons you've already thought of.

Follow up on vague answers. "It was too expensive" could mean the product price, the shipping fee, or a cheaper option elsewhere. A single "compared to what?" turns a vague answer into one you can act on.

Write down their exact words. Don't summarize "nobody answered my question about sizing" as "support issue." The shopper's words tell you what to fix, and they often become better product-page copy than anything you'd write yourself.

Keep it respectful. Ask once, keep it short, and only contact people through channels they agreed to.

Turning answers into a fix-first list

Once you have answers, make them usable:

  1. Group them. Put each answer under a short theme in plain words, such as "shipping cost surprise," "unsure it fits," "no reply to question" or "found it cheaper."
  2. Count each theme. The theme that comes up most often is your first fix, not the one that's most fun to work on.
  3. Write the smallest useful change. "Show the shipping cost on the product page" is a change you can ship this week. "Improve trust" is not.
  4. Ship it, then ask again. After the fix, keep asking the same question. If the top reason drops away, the next theme moves up the list.

Keep a short record of each theme, the change you made and the date. That gives you a history of what shoppers told you and what you changed in response, instead of a stack of one-off redesigns.

Frequently asked questions

What is the most common reason shoppers leave a Shopify store without buying?

It varies by store. Checkout friction, unexpected shipping costs, low trust and unanswered product questions are common causes, but the only reliable way to know your main reason is to ask the shoppers who left.

Can Shopify analytics tell me why shoppers leave?

No. Analytics shows where shoppers drop off, such as at the cart or at checkout. It doesn't record the reason, so you need to ask them.

What should I ask a shopper who didn't buy?

One open question, such as "What stopped you from ordering today?", followed by one clarifying question if the answer is vague. Write down their exact words.

Should I redesign my product page if people aren't buying?

Not first. Find out where shoppers drop off and why, then make the smallest change that fixes the most common reason.
